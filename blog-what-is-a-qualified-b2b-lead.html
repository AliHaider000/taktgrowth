What counts as a qualified B2B lead, and what agencies count instead

Most marketing reports count clicks, rankings and newsletter sign-ups. Here is the definition I use with clients, what it changed at ITCE, and a monthly check that keeps the numbers honest.

Between 19 June and 16 September 2026, visitors from organic search and AI assistants sent 423 forms through ITCE's website, up from 303 in the 90 days before. In the same period, confirmed leads went down, from 87 to 76. Both numbers came from the same website. Only one of them is work for a salesperson.

Most monthly reports would put the first number on page one and leave the second out. I show the client both, because the gap between them is where next quarter's work is. This article is about that gap: what I count as a qualified lead, what I do not, and the monthly routine that keeps the count honest.

What most marketing reports count

Open a typical agency report and you will find sessions, impressions, average position and a line called conversions. Ask what a conversion is and the answer is often a newsletter sign-up, a click on the phone number, a visit to the contact page or a scroll to the bottom of a page. None of these is a buyer asking for a price.

This is not always dishonest. Google Ads and GA4 count whatever you tell them to count, and many accounts were set up years ago by someone who wanted the graphs to move. The result is the same either way: marketing reports growth, sales sees the same inbox, and nobody can connect the two.

The Content Marketing Institute sees this across the industry: 64% of manufacturing marketers say they have difficulty tying their content to revenue (2024). Most of the time the problem starts one step earlier. What is being counted was never a buyer in the first place.

The definition I use with every client

I count a lead as qualified when two things are true. First, the action: an RFQ or quote request, a booked demo or site visit, a tracked sales call, or a distributor enquiry. Second, the sender: a company that fits the customer profile. Traffic, rankings and impressions never count, however good they look.

The customer profile does not need a workshop. One sentence is enough: which industries, which countries, what size of company, and what they would buy from you. A machine builder might write “food and packaging plants in Bulgaria and Romania with their own maintenance team”. Write it down before you start counting, so the labels do not depend on who does them that day.

Two rows cause most of the arguments. A datasheet download is a useful signal, and in my lead tracking playbook I mark it as a key event in GA4. But a key event is something you watch, not a lead you report. The 60-second rule for calls is mine, not a law: it filters out wrong numbers and hang-ups with very little effort. Pick a threshold, write it down and keep it.

Why more forms did not mean more leads at ITCE

Back to the numbers at the top. ITCE sells IT training and ServiceNow consulting from Sofia Tech Park. In the 90 days to 16 September 2026, its sessions from organic and AI search grew 17.6% and key events grew 28.4%. Forms sent rose 39.6%. Confirmed leads fell.

I do not know yet which of the extra forms came from buyers, and I am not going to guess in a report. Form counts can rise for reasons that have nothing to do with demand: a new form on a page that had none, more spam, job seekers using the contact form, existing clients asking for help. In GA4, all of that looks like growth.

So the next step at ITCE is not more traffic. It is a form audit, to see what each form actually collects, and a monthly reconciliation of form submissions against the CRM. If I reported 423 on its own, the quarter would look like a win of almost 40%. Next to 76, it is a to-do list.

What happened when Google Ads counted only real leads

The same client shows the other side. When I audited ITCE's Google Ads account, its conversion goals did not match what sales would call a lead. Google's bidding works towards whatever you set as a primary conversion. In Google's own help pages, primary actions are “used for bidding” and secondary actions are “for observation only”. If a page view or a button click is primary, the algorithm goes looking for more page views and button clicks.

I rebuilt the goals so that only real leads steer bidding. Anything that is only a signal belongs in secondary, where it still shows in reports but does not decide where the money goes. This was one fix among many: 520 irrelevant search terms blocked, 521 keywords moved from Broad to Phrase match, Display and Search Partners switched off, and the target cost per lead lowered from €32 to €15. Without clean goals, that target would have pointed at the wrong thing.

You cannot improve what you do not count

A private dental practice near Washington DC is far from industrial sales, but it shows the starting point I see most often: nothing was counted. No tracked forms, no tracked calls, no record of online booking requests. The website might have been bringing in patients, or not. Nobody could say.

Once forms, calls and booking requests were tracked, 821 enquiries showed up over 15 months: 360 booking requests, 243 forms and 218 phone calls. Only then did those numbers become something to improve, page by page.

The lesson for an industrial company is in the third bar. More than a quarter of the enquiries were phone calls. A buyer who needs a spare part this week is more likely to call than to fill in a form, so if you count only forms, you miss the most urgent buyers.

Setting this up takes an afternoon: Google Tag Manager and GA4 on every page, key events for RFQ sent, demo booked and phone number clicked, hidden form fields that save the source and landing page, and a tracked phone number for website visitors. The step-by-step version is in playbook 1: lead tracking.

A monthly reconciliation that fits in an hour

Tracking tells you how many forms and calls came in. Reconciliation tells you how many were real. I do it in the first week of every month, and the spreadsheet does not need to be clever.

Monthly lead reconciliation
  1. ExportDownload last month's form submissions, the call log from your tracked numbers and any Google Ads lead form entries. One row per enquiry: date, company, source, page.
  2. MatchCheck each row against your CRM or a shared spreadsheet. Merge duplicates: a buyer who sent a form and then called is one lead, not two.
  3. LabelGive every row one label: qualified, not qualified, spam or existing customer. Let someone from sales do it, using the written customer profile.
  4. ComparePut qualified leads next to forms sent and calls received, by source. The gap is your noise, and its size tells you where to look.
  5. Feed back to Google AdsGoogle Ads gives every ad click an ID (GCLID). Save it in a hidden form field and you can import the leads that turned out qualified as offline conversions. Google recommends the newer enhanced conversions for leads, which also uses the email address from the form.
  6. Fix one leakFix the biggest source of noise. If students use the RFQ form, put a careers link next to it. If suppliers pitch through it, give them a separate address.
The rule

Report qualified leads next to forms and calls every month. If only one number goes in the report, make it the smaller one.

With fewer than a hundred enquiries a month, this is an hour of work. After three months you will know your real cost per qualified lead by channel. That is the only number worth comparing agencies on, including me.

What to do this week

  1. Write your customer profile in one sentence and share it with sales.
  2. In Google Ads, open Goals, then Conversions, and check which actions are primary. Anything that is not a request from a buyer moves to secondary.
  3. Export last month's forms and calls and give each one of the four labels above.
  4. Check that your forms save the click ID and the landing page. If they do not, that is the first fix.

If you would rather set this up with someone who has done it before, it is the first thing I do with every new client.
