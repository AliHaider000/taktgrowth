Turn four days at a trade fair into a quarter of pipeline

The stand is the expensive part, and its value is decided in the minutes after each conversation. These are the six steps I use before and during the fair, with a checklist and a note template.

Day two of the fair, mid-afternoon. The maintenance manager of a bottling plant stops at your stand and asks whether you can add vision inspection to a line that is already running. The line stops for two weeks in July, and that is the only window. You talk for six minutes, he takes a brochure, you take his card. By closing time his card is somewhere in a stack of forty.

That card is where a trade fair makes or loses its money. The space, the stand and four days of your best people are paid for in advance. Whether any of it turns into quotes is mostly decided at the fair, not after it.

This is my trade show playbook: six steps for the weeks before the fair and the four days on the stand. For the 30 days after it, use the 30-day follow-up calendar. This article covers everything up to its first day.

What a stand can do, and what it cannot

MachTech & InnoTech 2026 ran for four days at Inter Expo Center in Sofia, 6 to 9 October, with 205 exhibitors in its catalogue. A buyer walking two halls in an afternoon passes dozens of stands that look like yours. In my experience, buyers rarely choose a supplier at the stand. They take the card back to the office and decide there.

Engineers say the same: in TREW Marketing's 2025 survey, a well-kept website scored 4.0 out of 5 for credibility and trade show sponsorship 2.8. The stand earns you a conversation. The decision comes later, on your website and in your email.

So the stand has three jobs: start good conversations, record each one before it fades, and send each one to a clear next step. The six steps cover all three.

The playbook in six steps
  1. Before you goA page for show visitors, with its QR code on the stand and your cards.
  2. Within five minutesA note for every conversation: who, what they need, how urgent.
  3. Within 48 hoursA personal email that mentions the actual conversation.
  4. In every emailOne concrete next step: a quote, a sample, a visit or a call.
  5. In the CRMThe show name on every contact, so you can count the result.
  6. After the showThe questions you heard most, turned into website pages.

Step 1: build one page for show visitors before you go

The people you talk to will look you up again, and so will the colleague they forward your card to. Give them a page made for that moment, not your homepage, at an address short enough to type from a card, such as yourcompany.com/machtech. On it:

  • What you are showing: each machine or product on the stand, with its key specs as text, not only in a PDF.
  • A short video of the machine running in your own workshop. One or two minutes is enough.
  • A quote form that accepts a drawing and asks only what you need to quote.
  • Hall, stand number and dates, and the direct phone numbers of the people on the stand.

Put the page's QR code on the stand, on your cards and on anything printed you hand out. Point it at a link tagged with the show's name, so those visits appear in GA4 under that name, and test it on three phones before printing. The page follows the same rules as a capability page that wins RFQs.

The common mistake is a QR code that opens the homepage, or a fair page that goes live a week after the fair. A page chosen for a specific visitor does better than a general one: when I audited ITCE's Google Ads account, leads from landing pages ITCE had chosen cost €23.50, against €47.59 from pages Google picked.

Step 2: note every conversation within five minutes

Five minutes, because the next visitor is already waiting. Three conversations later, the man with the bottling line has become “someone who asked about cameras”. By the evening, he is a card.

Write down name, company, what they need and how urgent it is, in the visitor's own words where you can. “Interested” is not a note. “Vision check on a running bottling line, only window is a two week stop in July, wants a site visit” is.

A phone note beats a badge scan, because a scan records who stopped by, not what they asked. Put one simple form on every phone on the stand, photograph the card into the same entry, and have one person check at lunch and at closing that every card has a note.

Steps 3 and 4: a personal email within 48 hours, with one next step

In my experience, many companies take more than two days to send the first email after a fair. They wait for the fair to end, for the stand to come back, then for Monday. By then the buyer has met your competitors too, and some of them wrote first.

So follow up during the fair. Conversations from day 1 get their email that evening or the next morning, before the stand opens. With a good note in front of you, a personal email takes a few minutes.

Mention the actual conversation. “Thanks for visiting our stand” gets ignored, because it could have gone to anyone. “You said the line stops for two weeks in July. If we see it running before then, we can tell you whether the retrofit fits that window” could only go to one person.

Then offer one concrete next step: a quote, a sample, a factory visit or a call. One, not three. “Let us know if you would like a quote, a sample or a visit” hands the buyer a decision. “Would Tuesday or Thursday next week suit you for a visit?” asks for a yes. Better still, agree that step at the stand before the visitor leaves, write it in the note, and let the email simply confirm it.

The 48-hour test

Every conversation from the stand gets a personal email and one agreed next step within 48 hours, sent while the fair is still running. If it waits for Monday, it is already late.

From the last day of the fair, the 30-day calendar takes over: what you promised, the calls, the follow-ups and the count on day 30. If you followed up during the fair, its first emails are already sent.

Step 5: tag every contact with the show name

Three months after the fair, someone will ask whether the stand was worth it. Without a tag the answer is a guess, and next year's budget gets set on that guess.

Create the tag in the CRM before the show and spell it once, for example “MachTech 2026”. Every stand contact gets it on the day it is entered. Use the same name in the QR code link and as an answer to “how did you hear about us” on your quote form, so an RFQ sent through the website two months later counts too. Tracking every RFQ back to the search shows how.

At 90 days, count qualified leads, quotes sent and orders won with the show's tag, using the definition of a qualified lead; badge scans are not leads. Count at 90 days rather than 30, because a quote from a fair often becomes an order months later.

Step 6: turn the questions you heard most into website pages

Visitors ask at the stand what they could not find on your website, which makes the stand the cheapest keyword research you will ever do. If ten visitors asked something in four days, many more buyers are typing it into Google or asking an AI assistant.

Keep a tally sheet on the stand and add a mark each time a question comes up. On the last evening, the top five questions are your next five pages.

Before writing, check in Search Console how buyers phrase each question, as in finding the searches that come before a quote request. Then answer it plainly, with numbers, one page per question. Clear, factual pages are also what AI assistants quote when a buyer asks them for suppliers; getting named by AI assistants covers that side.

What to do this week

If you exhibit in the next two months, build the show page and the note form now. Both are cheap to make and worth nothing if they arrive after the fair.

If your last fair is already behind you, tag the contacts you have, list the questions you heard most, and start the 30-day calendar today.
